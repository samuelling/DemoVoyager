Where this comes from: the demoscene

In the 1980s, software crackers on the Commodore 64 and the Amiga signed their work with short intros: a logo, a scrolling message, a loop of chip music. The intros became a contest of their own, and by the early 1990s groups were making demos with no game attached at all, real-time pieces of music and moving pictures whose only purpose was to show what a machine could do in the right hands. On the PC, Future Crew’s Second Reality, released at Assembly in 1993, set the standard for a generation: spaceships, vector objects, a tracker score, all running live on a home computer.

Demos are released at demoparties such as Assembly in Finland (since 1992), The Party in Denmark and, today, Revision in Germany, where the audience votes on the competitions. The culture has its own conventions: groups instead of companies, handles instead of names, a coder, a musician and a graphician in the credits, greetings to rival groups, scrollers boasting that no 3D card is required. Finland added the demoscene to its national inventory of living heritage in 2020, and Germany followed in 2021.

The music came from trackers. Ultimate Soundtracker on the Amiga (1987), and later Scream Tracker and FastTracker on the PC, laid songs out as numbered rows of notes in a few channels, played pattern by pattern from an order list. A tracker song is music written as data, readable and editable by eye, which is why the 40K tier here writes a real tracker module.

1980s cracktros→1987 Soundtracker→1992 Assembly→1993 Second Reality→64K and 4K intros→2009 Elevated→2020 living heritage

The 4K intro: the limit is the point

Alongside full demos, parties run size-limited competitions: 64K intros, 4K intros, and smaller still (1K, 256 bytes). A 4K intro must fit everything in 4,096 bytes, the program, the music and the pictures, with no data files to lean on. Nothing can be stored, so everything is computed: textures grown from noise, instruments synthesized from a few oscillators, landscapes from formulas, the whole file squeezed by compressing linkers written for the purpose. Farbrausch’s 64K intro .the .product (2000) and RGBA and TBC’s 4K intro Elevated (2009), a flight over sunlit mountains raymarched from a distance function, showed how far a few kilobytes of generative code could reach.

A size limit works like the fourteen lines of a sonnet: it does not stop invention, it forces it. Every byte has to earn its place, so the work becomes a set of rules that unfold into something much larger than themselves. That is the idea this project borrows. Each Demoseed is a 4K-style generator: a seed and a set of rules, with no stored music or images. Orders of Magnitude then asks the question in reverse. Give the same generative idea ten times the room, then ten times again, each step a later era of hardware and taste, and watch what the extra room is spent on: richer sound, richer pictures and, in Free form, richer structure.

One number in, a whole demo out

Everything starts from a 32-bit seed. A hash splits it into independent random streams, one each for the names, the composition, the instruments, the scene order, the visuals and the free-form plans, so changing how one part is generated never disturbs the others. The same seed produces the same song and the same pictures on any machine.

seed→composition→form (shared, free or era idioms)→score: notes with times→era synth+scenes chosen by section role

The composer every tier shares

From 40K up the seed first goes through one composer. It picks a mode (aeolian, dorian, phrygian, harmonic minor, ionian or mixolydian, weighted toward the minor ones), a key, a tempo between 112 and 152 bpm, and two four-chord progressions from curated lists, filtered so no chord is diminished. It then writes four one-bar motifs: onsets are drawn against a metric template that favours downbeats, and each step moves mostly one or two scale degrees. A phrase shape such as AABA strings the motifs into four-bar phrases. On every beat the melody is pulled to the nearest chord tone, and the last note of a phrase lands on the root. The composer also picks a drum style, a bass rhythm and an arpeggio style.

The 40K tier writes all this into a six-channel tracker module, 64 rows per pattern, in a form such as intro, build, A, A2, break, B, B2, A3, outro. The 4K tier is the exception: its file is too small to hold this composer, so it carries its own four-chord, two-motif miniature.

Shared form: one song, arranged or recomposed

In Shared form the larger tiers keep the 40K song and spend their allowance on treatment. 400K plays every section twice and varies the repeat with passing tones, an octave lift at the phrase end, a busier bass and a new fill. 4M recomposes the material as a big-room track (intro, verse, build, drop, break, build, drop, outro). 40M recomposes it as a sonata-like piece: theme B in the relative key, a development that walks the circle of fifths with a canon, and a coda ending on a major chord.

Free form: each tier writes its own structure

In Free form the material stays the same but each tier plans its own form, as far as its allowance permits.

TierLevelsThemesTransformations a return may carryKeys and meters

4M2: parts › sections3up to 2, adding: transposed, fragmented, augmented, reharmonised, half-time, in thirdsrelative, subdominant, a lift; 4/4
40M3: movements › sections › phrases5up to 3, adding: inverted, retrograde, diminished, canon, modal mixture; two themes in counterpointsix key areas; 3/4, 7/8, 5/4 in development, variation and break

A plan is a short layout string such as AtB|xxb|cBA. Capital letters are themes: the first appearance is a statement, later ones are returns that draw random transformations from the tier’s list. Lower-case letters are functions: t bridge, x development, b build, d drop, c climax, k break, v variation. Bars between | form a part (4M) or a movement (40M), and at 40M long sections split again into phrases of 4 to 8 bars in patterns like aba or aab. Each tier chooses among several such layouts (rondo-like, sonata-like, arch, theme and variations, build and drop), so forms differ from seed to seed.

Each section gets an energy level: a base value for its role, plus a swell that peaks about 62% of the way through the piece. Builds and bridges take the key of whatever follows them.

Era idioms: structure in the habits of its time

Free form scales how much structure a tier may use, but its vocabulary (rondo, sonata, inversion, canon) comes from the conservatory, not from the scene. Era idioms keep the same material and give each tier the structural habits of its own era of demoscene music.

TierEraHow the form is built
40K1997 trackerAlready idiomatic: a few patterns reused from an order list, channels switched in and out, an echo channel, a key lift for the last chorus.
400K2003 trance and progressiveLayering is the structure. Every 8 bars one part enters or leaves: kick, hats, bass, arpeggio, lead, pad. A breakdown strips the drums, a snare roll builds, the climax brings every layer back, the outro removes them one by one.
4M2012 EDMBuild and drop. Verse hooks as call and answer between lead and keys; a build with a gap before the drop; a big-room or half-time drop; a second drop that either switches style or lifts the key.
40M2026 cinematic scoringActs, not verses: opening, Act I, Act II, Act III, epilogue. Leitmotifs change mood with the story (statement, darker, threat, heroic, remembered), ostinatos and drones carry tension, hits mark the cuts, two motifs meet at the climax, and the last statement returns slowed down.

These are broad strokes of a varied scene, chosen as each era’s most recognisable habits rather than a complete survey. Tempo changes, common in film-style scores, are not modelled; the 40M tier suggests them with augmented and diminished rhythms instead.

Turning a plan into notes

  • Harmony. One chord per bar from the theme’s progression. A relative-key section re-centres it, a reharmonised section swaps in a second progression, a development walks the circle of fifths, a build sits on the dominant, and statements and returns close with V–I.
  • Melody. The theme’s motifs over that harmony. Transformations act here: augmented doubles every duration, diminished halves them, inverted flips each interval, retrograde plays the phrase backwards, fragmented keeps the first notes of each motif and sequences them, canon adds a second voice two beats behind and a third below, counterpoint lays theme B’s melody over theme A’s harmony.
  • Texture follows energy. Low energy gets pads, a sub bass and sparse melody; middle energy adds a pulse or walking bass, arpeggios and a light groove; high energy brings four-on-the-floor or the full groove, an octave bass, stabs (4M) or choir and a counter-melody (40M).
  • Meters. Drum patterns follow the bar: 3/4 as three beats, 7/8 grouped 2+2+3, 5/4 grouped 3+2.

Measuring structure from the notes

The plan says what the form should be. The measurements ask what a listener could actually hear, using only the notes in the score.

Bar features. Each bar becomes a 48-number profile: the melody’s scale degrees relative to the section’s key (12), its intervals from one note to the next, −7 to +7 semitones (15), where in the bar its notes start (8), the accompaniment’s scale degrees at lower weight (12), and drum density (1). Scale degrees and intervals mean a return in another key still matches, the way a listener recognises a transposed tune.

Similarity map. The cosine similarity of every pair of bar profiles. The picture is smoothed along the diagonal over three bars, the usual way to make repeated passages show as stripes. The statistics use the raw values.

d′. Take every pair of themed sections (intros, outros, builds and bridges are left out), line them up from their starts, and collect the bar-for-bar similarities. Pairs on the same theme go in one pool and pairs on different themes in the other. Then:

d′ = (mean_same − mean_different) / √((var_same + var_different) / 2)

Above about 1.5 the form is plain to hear. Between 0.7 and 1.5 it is audible but disguised. Below that the transformations largely hide it.

Era signatures. Three measures separate idioms where d′ cannot. Layers counts the parts playing in each bar (kick, hats, snare, bass, arpeggio, pad, lead, counter-melody); a trance form shows as a staircase up and down. Repeats is the share of bars that nearly repeat an earlier bar (similarity 0.9 or more): high for loop-built tracker and trance music, lower for through-composed scores. Phrase is the average section length in bars.

Recognisable. For each return, the mean bar-for-bar similarity to its theme’s first appearance.

Chords. Estimated per bar by matching the accompaniment’s pitch classes, with the bass weighted up, against every major, minor and diminished triad, then named against the section’s key.

What the numbers can and cannot say

  • The features are a simple model of recognition, not a listener. Timbre, register and loudness are ignored, so a theme moved from oboe to bells still counts as itself.
  • Lining sections up from their starts penalises augmentation, diminution and retrograde. That is partly the point, since those are harder to hear, but the model may judge them more harshly than an attentive listener would.
  • d′ rests on few sections and varies from seed to seed. Compare tiers over several seeds before drawing conclusions.
  • The 4K song is not measured: it is generated inside the packed 4K file by its own composer.

Budgets

4K, 40K and 400K must fit their file in the limit. At 4M and 40M the code is free, and everything the demo builds while it runs must fit: render targets, textures, noise volumes, particle state, reverb impulse responses and sample banks. The 4M tier renders at 800×450 to stay under 4 MB; the 40M tier spends about 18 MB on physically modelled instruments and the rest on simulation buffers.
